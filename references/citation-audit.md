# Citation Audit

Use this reference when the professor requests substantive citation review.

The citation audit is professor-facing. It diagnoses whether legal propositions are supported by appropriate authority. It is not full Bluebook, OSCOLA, or Chinese citation-style editing by default.

## Required Audit Categories

Classify citation issues into these categories:

1. Missing authority: a legal proposition appears without a citation.
2. Weak authority: the source is secondary, policy-oriented, or descriptive when primary law is required.
3. Authority mismatch: the cited source supports a narrower, different, or merely descriptive proposition.
4. Pinpoint defect: the citation lacks the page, paragraph, article, decision number, or other location needed for verification.
5. Source hierarchy defect: the paper treats treaty text, customary international law, cases, COP decisions, committee findings, ILC conclusions, scholarship, and policy reports as interchangeable.
6. Currency defect: the cited legal position has been superseded, reversed, narrowed, or materially changed.
7. Form defect affecting credibility: spelling, case name, journal, publisher, date, or cross-reference errors suggest lack of source control.

## Required Issue Format

For each concrete citation issue, include:

- Location in the paper.
- Proposition being supported.
- Citation used or missing.
- Diagnosis.
- Needed repair.

## Full Paper Expectation

For full papers with citation review requested, actively look for 10 concrete citation issues or explain why fewer are visible from the submitted material.

Do not invent citation issues to satisfy the number. If the available text lacks footnotes, say that the audit is limited to visible source selection, source hierarchy, and legal framing.

## Legal Authority Discipline

When assessing citation support, distinguish:

- Primary legal authority from secondary literature.
- Binding law from persuasive materials.
- Positive law from policy argument.
- Institutional output from state consent.
- Technical expertise from legal interpretive authority.

Treat scholarly support as scholarship, not as primary legal authority.
