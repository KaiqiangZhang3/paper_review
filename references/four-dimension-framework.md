# Four-Dimension Assessment Framework

This framework applies to **all papers reviewed by this skill**: master's papers, doctoral dissertations, thesis chapters, and peer-review manuscripts.

These four dimensions supplement the standard review dimensions and mode standards. They assess the paper's **scholarly self-awareness**—whether the author understands what they are doing, why, and how it fits into existing scholarship.

## Application by Paper Type

- **Doctoral dissertations**: Apply all four dimensions at full rigor
- **Peer-review manuscripts**: Apply all four dimensions at full rigor (contribution and methodology are central to publishability)
- **Master's papers**: Apply all four dimensions with adjusted expectations based on program level
- **Coursework papers**: Apply selectively, focusing on Dimensions 1 (Problem Scope) and 3 (Contribution), with relaxed standards

---

## Dimension 1: Problem Scope and Scale (多大的事儿)

### Question to Ask

**Can the paper articulate what problem or theoretical question it addresses, and at what scale?**

### Assessment Criteria

The paper should clearly answer:

1. **What is the problem?**
   - Is it a doctrinal gap, interpretive disagreement, institutional puzzle, normative inconsistency, or empirical phenomenon?
   - Is the problem statement specific enough to be answerable within the paper scope?

2. **How significant is the problem?**
   - Does it affect treaty interpretation, state practice, institutional design, scholarly debate, or policy?
   - Is the significance asserted or demonstrated?

3. **What are the boundaries?**
   - Is the problem scoped to specific treaties, regions, time periods, actors, or legal regimes?
   - Does the paper acknowledge what it does NOT address?

### Common Defects

- **Scope inflation**: The problem is stated so broadly ("the legitimacy crisis of international law") that the paper cannot meaningfully address it.
- **Unstated boundaries**: The thesis shifts between different problems without acknowledging the shift (e.g., doctrinal analysis → normative theory → institutional reform).
- **Significance assertion without demonstration**: The thesis claims the problem is "urgent" or "fundamental" but does not show why.
- **Problem-solution mismatch**: The stated problem is doctrinal, but the paper offers policy recommendations; or the problem is normative, but the paper provides only descriptive analysis.

### Diagnostic Format

> **Chapter 1, ¶3-5**: The thesis states it addresses "the problem of sovereignty in international law" but does not specify whether this is a conceptual, doctrinal, institutional, or normative problem. The scope is too broad for a single thesis to address meaningfully. The boundaries between sovereignty as legal doctrine, political theory, and institutional design are not drawn. **Serious defect** — the paper should specify which dimension of sovereignty it addresses and why that dimension is significant.

## Dimension 2: Methodological Path and Alternatives (脉络选择)

### Question to Ask

**Can the paper explain in which intellectual or methodological framework it operates, whether alternatives exist, and why the chosen path is appropriate?**

### Assessment Criteria

The paper should address:

1. **What is the chosen path?**
   - Doctrinal (treaty interpretation, customary law formation, source hierarchy)?
   - Theoretical (constructivist, realist, critical, feminist, Third World approaches)?
   - Empirical (case studies, statistical analysis, archival research)?
   - Normative (moral philosophy, legitimacy theory, justice-based argument)?
   - Institutional (design analysis, comparative institutional assessment)?

2. **Are alternatives visible?**
   - Does the paper acknowledge that the same problem could be addressed through different methodologies?
   - Examples: A treaty interpretation problem could be approached through textual analysis, object-and-purpose reasoning, travaux préparatoires, subsequent practice, or comparative law analogy.

3. **Why this path?**
   - Does the paper justify the chosen methodology by showing its fit with the problem, its advantages over alternatives, or its capacity to generate the desired contribution?
   - Or does the paper simply adopt a methodology without reflection?

### Common Defects

- **Methodological silence**: The thesis never states its method, as if the approach were self-evident or universal.
- **Method-problem mismatch**: The thesis uses doctrinal analysis to address a problem that requires normative theory, or uses empirical methods without explaining how empirical findings generate legal conclusions.
- **Unacknowledged alternatives**: The thesis treats its chosen path as the only possible one, ignoring competing methodologies in the same field.
- **Borrowed methodology without adaptation**: The thesis imports a method from another discipline (economics, sociology, philosophy) without explaining how it applies to international law.

### Diagnostic Format

> **Chapter 2, methodological section**: The thesis states it will use "a doctrinal approach" but does not explain what this means in the context of customary international law formation, where doctrine, state practice, opinio juris, and institutional practice intersect. Alternative approaches—such as behavioral analysis of state practice or constructivist accounts of norm emergence—are not mentioned. The thesis does not justify why doctrinal analysis is the appropriate method for a problem that involves contested state practice. **Serious defect** — the paper should specify its methodological framework and explain why it fits the research question.

## Dimension 3: Contribution and Challenge (贡献/挑战)

### Question to Ask

**Can the paper articulate what it contributes or challenges in existing scholarship, doctrine, or practice?**

### Assessment Criteria

The paper should specify:

1. **What does the paper contribute?**
   - New doctrinal interpretation (e.g., a novel reading of treaty language or customary rule)?
   - Theoretical framework (e.g., a new way to understand fragmentation, legitimacy, or compliance)?
   - Empirical finding (e.g., patterns in state practice previously unnoticed)?
   - Normative argument (e.g., a justice-based critique of current doctrine)?
   - Institutional design proposal (e.g., reform of dispute settlement mechanisms)?

2. **What does the paper challenge?**
   - A dominant scholarly position (e.g., "Scholars assume X, but this thesis shows Y")?
   - Received doctrinal wisdom (e.g., "Courts interpret provision A as B, but this thesis argues C")?
   - Policy consensus (e.g., "The current regime assumes X works, but this thesis demonstrates it fails")?

3. **Is the contribution or challenge clearly stated?**
   - Does the paper articulate its contribution in the introduction and return to it in the conclusion?
   - Or does the contribution remain implicit, requiring the examiner to infer it?

4. **Is the contribution commensurate with the paper scope?**
   - Does the paper claim to "revolutionize" understanding but offer only incremental refinement?
   - Or does it modestly claim "a case study" when it actually provides broader theoretical insight?

### Common Defects

- **Contribution by topic selection**: The thesis claims originality simply because it addresses an under-studied treaty or region, without showing what new understanding it generates.
- **Implicit contribution**: The thesis does solid work but never explicitly states what it contributes. The reader must infer whether the contribution is doctrinal, theoretical, empirical, or normative.
- **Overstated challenge**: The thesis claims to "refute" a position but only offers an alternative interpretation or edge-case qualification.
- **Understated contribution**: The thesis modestly describes itself as "exploring" or "examining" when it actually advances a novel claim that challenges existing scholarship.
- **Contribution without demonstration**: The thesis asserts in the introduction that it will "contribute to understanding X" but never returns to that claim in the conclusion or shows how the argument delivers the promised contribution.

### Diagnostic Format

> **Chapter 1, ¶8 and Chapter 6, conclusion**: The thesis claims to "contribute to the debate on international criminal responsibility" but does not specify whether the contribution is doctrinal (a new interpretation of Article 25), theoretical (a new framework for responsibility), or normative (a critique of existing allocations). The introduction promises to "challenge the dominant view" but does not name the view or its proponents. The conclusion restates findings but does not return to the stated contribution. **Serious defect** — the paper should explicitly state whether it contributes a doctrinal refinement, theoretical framework, empirical finding, or normative critique, and demonstrate that contribution in the body and conclusion.

## Dimension 4: Engagement with Adverse Scholarship (学术挑战的处理)

### Question to Ask

**Does the paper identify scholarship that challenges its claims, and does it address those challenges directly?**

### Assessment Criteria

The paper should demonstrate:

1. **Identification of adverse scholarship**
   - Which scholars hold positions contrary to the thesis's argument?
   - Are these positions accurately characterized?
   - Does the paper engage with the strongest contrary scholarship, or only with weaker, more easily refuted positions?

2. **Direct engagement**
   - Does the paper address adverse scholarship by:
     - Showing that it misreads sources?
     - Demonstrating that it relies on outdated evidence?
     - Arguing that it applies an inappropriate methodology?
     - Conceding partial validity but distinguishing the thesis's scope or claim?
   - Or does the paper simply cite contrary views in passing without substantive response?

3. **Integration into argument**
   - Is adverse scholarship addressed in a dedicated "literature review" chapter that is disconnected from the thesis's analytical chapters?
   - Or is it integrated into the substantive analysis, showing how the thesis's approach resolves problems that other scholars leave unresolved?

### Common Defects

- **Strawman engagement**: The thesis engages only with weak or outdated contrary positions while ignoring the strongest recent scholarship.
- **Citation without engagement**: The thesis lists contrary positions in a literature review but never responds to them substantively in the analytical chapters.
- **Evasion through scope**: The thesis avoids adverse scholarship by claiming "this is outside the scope of this thesis" when the contrary position directly threatens the thesis's central claim.
- **Aggressive dismissal without argument**: The thesis dismisses contrary scholarship as "unconvincing" or "flawed" without showing why.
- **Failure to identify adverse scholarship**: The thesis presents its position as uncontested when significant scholarly disagreement exists.

### Diagnostic Format

> **Chapter 2, literature review**: The thesis cites Scholars A, B, and C as holding contrary positions on treaty interpretation methodology. However, these citations appear only in the literature review. In Chapters 3-5, where the paper applies its own interpretive approach, these contrary positions are never revisited. The thesis does not explain why its approach is superior, how it responds to the objections raised by contrary scholarship, or whether the thesis's contribution lies in resolving a methodological dispute. **Serious defect** — the paper should integrate engagement with adverse scholarship into its analytical chapters, showing how its approach addresses problems that other methods leave unresolved.

## Integration with Standard Review Dimensions

These four dimensions supplement, not replace, the standard review dimensions:

- **Dimension 1 (Problem Scope)** supplements "Research Question and Central Claim"
- **Dimension 2 (Methodological Path)** adds a new dimension not covered elsewhere
- **Dimension 3 (Contribution/Challenge)** deepens "Literature Engagement and Scholarly Contribution"
- **Dimension 4 (Adverse Scholarship)** extends "Adverse-Authority Stress Test" from legal sources to scholarly literature

## Application Rules

### When to Apply

**Apply to all papers**, but adjust rigor and expectations:

#### Doctoral Dissertations and Thesis Chapters
- **Full rigor** on all four dimensions
- These are core expectations for doctoral-level work
- Defects in any dimension are serious

#### Peer-Review Manuscripts
- **Full rigor** on Dimensions 2 (Methodology), 3 (Contribution), and 4 (Adverse Scholarship)
- These dimensions are central to publishability assessment
- Editors and reviewers will ask these questions
- Dimension 1 (Problem Scope) may be more relaxed for short articles

#### Master's Papers
- **Moderate expectations** adjusted to program level
- Dimension 1 (Problem Scope): Expected but scope can be narrower
- Dimension 2 (Methodology): Basic awareness expected, not full justification
- Dimension 3 (Contribution): Contribution at master's level (not originality requirement)
- Dimension 4 (Adverse Scholarship): Basic engagement expected, not comprehensive response

#### Coursework Papers and Short Essays
- **Selective application** focusing on Dimensions 1 and 3
- Dimension 1: Problem clarity expected
- Dimension 3: Contribution awareness encouraged but not required
- Dimensions 2 and 4: Apply only if paper claims scholarly contribution

### How to Apply

1. After completing standard structure reconstruction, assess these four dimensions in a dedicated section titled:
   - **Doctoral/peer-review**: "Four-Dimension Assessment" (Chinese: "四维评估")
   - **Master's**: "Scholarly Awareness Assessment" (Chinese: "学术意识评估")
   - **Coursework**: Integrate into "Main Problems" if significant

2. For each dimension, provide:
   - Whether the paper addresses the dimension explicitly or implicitly
   - Strengths (where the paper demonstrates self-awareness)
   - Defects (where the paper lacks clarity or reflection)
   - Severity (fatal / serious / minor), adjusted to paper type

3. Do not force all four dimensions if the material does not permit assessment. For example, if reviewing only a single chapter, state that contribution assessment is limited because the full paper is not visible.

## Example Integration into Review Memorandum

After the standard sections (Structure Reconstruction, Overall Judgment, Core Defect Snapshot, Main Problems), add:

```markdown
## Four-Dimension Assessment

### Problem Scope and Scale

[Assessment of whether the paper clearly defines what problem it addresses and at what scale]

### Methodological Path and Alternatives

[Assessment of whether the paper justifies its chosen methodology and acknowledges alternatives]

### Contribution and Challenge

[Assessment of whether the paper clearly states what it contributes or challenges]

### Engagement with Adverse Scholarship

[Assessment of whether the paper identifies and responds to contrary scholarly positions]
```

This section should be **concise and diagnostic**, not a separate full review. Identify 1-2 key strengths or defects per dimension.

---

## Length Rule by Paper Type

- **Doctoral dissertations**: 400-600 words (similar to "Main Problems" section)
- **Peer-review manuscripts**: 300-400 words
- **Master's papers**: 200-300 words
- **Thesis chapters**: 200-300 words
- **Coursework papers**: Integrate into "Main Problems" (no separate section)

Do not expand this section into a standalone methodological treatise. Keep it focused on diagnosing whether the paper demonstrates scholarly self-awareness.
