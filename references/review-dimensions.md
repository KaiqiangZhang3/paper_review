# Review Dimensions

Apply these dimensions after structure reconstruction. Weight them according to the selected mode.

## 1. Research Question and Central Claim

Assess whether the paper has a specific research question and a central claim that answers it. Identify whether the paper argues, describes, surveys, or proposes.

Look for:

- A question that is neither too broad nor already answered by the framing.
- A thesis that appears early enough for the reader to follow the analysis.
- A claim that can be tested against legal materials.
- Consistency between title, introduction, roadmap, and conclusion.

## 2. International Law Sources and Legal Materials

Assess the authority, relevance, and treatment of legal materials.

Use `legal-assessment-tests.md` when a claim requires three-layer legal assessment, claim-strength calibration, adverse-authority testing, comparative transfer analysis, or institution differentiation.

Primary public international law sources include:

- Treaties and treaty interpretation materials.
- Customary international law.
- General principles of law.
- International and regional court or tribunal decisions.
- State practice.
- Opinio juris.
- International organization materials.
- Soft law instruments.
- Draft articles, reports, conclusions, and guidelines where their legal status is correctly described.

For international economic law, consider whether trade, investment, sanctions, financial, or institutional materials are treated within their specific regimes.

For international criminal law, consider whether cases, elements of crimes, modes of liability, jurisdiction, admissibility, and institutional practice are handled accurately.

When the paper discusses international bodies, apply institution differentiation. Do not treat treaty bodies, COP/MOP decisions, compliance committees, expert committees, technical bodies, and courts as interchangeable. Control source hierarchy throughout the assessment.

## 3. Legal Reasoning

Assess how the paper moves from authority to conclusion.

Use three-layer legal assessment and claim-strength calibration when evaluating central claims. Distinguish legal authority from policy desirability and do not allow a normative preference to masquerade as positive law.

Look for:

- Distinction between lex lata, lex ferenda, policy argument, and normative theory.
- Accurate handling of source hierarchy.
- Clear explanation of why cited materials support the proposition.
- Treatment of ambiguity, contestation, or fragmentation when relevant.
- Avoidance of unsupported claims that "international law requires" or "states agree" without evidence.

## 4. Literature Engagement and Scholarly Contribution

Assess whether the paper knows the scholarly conversation it enters.

Scholarship is not primary legal authority. Treat scholarly sources as literature support or persuasive analysis, not as substitutes for treaties, custom, cases, state practice, opinio juris, or other primary legal materials.

Look for:

- Engagement with leading and recent scholarship.
- Accurate characterization of existing positions.
- A contribution that is more than topic selection.
- Explanation of what the paper changes: doctrine, theory, method, interpretation, institutional design, or normative evaluation.

## 5. Structure, Organization, and Writing Clarity

Assess whether structure serves the argument.

Look for:

- Introduction that states the problem, thesis, contribution, and roadmap.
- Sections arranged in an order that advances analysis.
- Headings that reflect argumentative work rather than generic topics.
- Paragraphs with clear topic sentences.
- Conclusions that synthesize rather than repeat.

## 6. Substantive Citation Quality

Use this dimension when the professor requests citation review.

Assess:

- Whether sources are authoritative for the propositions they support.
- Whether pinpoint references are available where needed.
- Whether citations are used as evidence rather than decoration.
- Whether footnotes hide important analysis that belongs in the text.
- Whether obvious format defects interfere with verification.

Do not conduct full Bluebook, OSCOLA, or Chinese citation-style editing by default.

## 7. Counterarguments, Adverse Authorities, Contrary Examples, and Limits

Use this dimension when the professor requests this check.

Use adverse-authority stress testing when requested. Identify the strongest objections to the thesis, not merely objections already mentioned by the paper.

Assess whether the paper handles:

- The strongest legal objection to the thesis.
- Adverse cases, contrary state practice, or contrary institutional materials.
- Competing doctrinal interpretations.
- Limits in method, scope, evidence, and generalizability.
- Alternative explanations for the same legal development.

If the paper is one-sided in a way that undermines the thesis, identify that even when this dimension was not requested as a separate deep check.

## Location Discipline

When diagnosing main problems, tie each problem to the most precise available location in the submitted material: page, section, heading, paragraph number, footnote number, or extracted paragraph marker. If page numbers are unavailable from Word/PDF extraction, use headings and paragraph markers instead. Do not present the main problem list as abstract advice when the submitted text contains locatable defects.
